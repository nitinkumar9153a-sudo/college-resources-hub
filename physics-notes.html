1 UNIT 1 - Wave Optics and Lasers

1. Huygens Principle

Definition:

Huygens principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets which spread in all directions with the speed of light.

Main Points

  • Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets.
  • Secondary wavelets travel with the velocity of the wave.
  • The new wavefront is the common tangent to the secondary wavelets.
Secondary wavelets ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ -------------------------- Old Wavefront → → → → → → → → → → → → New Wavefront __________________________
Exam Point: Huygens principle is used to explain reflection, refraction and diffraction of waves.

2. Superposition of Waves

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement at any point is equal to the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

y = y₁ + y₂

Superposition of coherent light waves produces the phenomenon of interference.

⭐ Exam Focus

Definition + principle + mathematical expression can be asked as a short-answer question.

3. Interference of Light

Interference is the phenomenon of redistribution of light intensity produced due to superposition of two coherent light waves.

Conditions for Sustained Interference

  • Sources must be coherent.
  • Sources should have the same frequency.
  • There should be a constant phase difference.
  • Amplitudes should be comparable.

Constructive Interference

When two waves meet in the same phase, intensity becomes maximum and a bright fringe is produced.

Path difference = nλ

Destructive Interference

When two waves meet in opposite phase, intensity becomes minimum and a dark fringe is produced.

Path difference = (2n + 1)λ/2
Wave 1: ~~~~~~~~ ~~~~~~~~ ↓ Wave 2: ~~~~~~~~ ~~~~~~~~ ↓ SUPERPOSITION ↓ Result: ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Bright → Constructive Dark → Destructive

4. Interference by Division of Wavefront

In division of wavefront, a single wavefront is divided into two or more parts which act as coherent sources.

Important examples include Young's double-slit experiment and Fresnel's biprism.

S * / \ / \ / \ S₁ S₂ \ / \ / \ / \ / Screen | | | | | | | | | | | Bright and dark fringes

5. Interference by Division of Amplitude

In division of amplitude, the amplitude of a single light beam is divided into two or more parts by partial reflection and transmission.

Examples include thin films, Newton's rings and Michelson interferometer.

6. Newton's Rings

Newton's rings are concentric circular bright and dark rings produced due to interference between the rays reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of a thin air film.

Plano-convex lens _________ / \ / \ / \ ----------------------- Air film ----------------------- Glass plate ↓ reflected light ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ ◎ Rings

Applications

  • Determination of wavelength of light.
  • Determination of radius of curvature.
  • Determination of refractive index of liquid.
Remember: Newton's rings are circular fringes caused by interference in a thin air film.

7. Fraunhofer Diffraction from a Single Slit

Diffraction is the bending or spreading of waves when they pass through a narrow aperture or around an obstacle.

Condition for Minima

a sin θ = mλ

Here a is slit width, λ is wavelength and m is the order of minimum.

8. Diffraction Grating

A diffraction grating consists of a large number of equally spaced parallel slits separated by opaque spaces.

d sin θ = nλ

Here d is grating element, θ is diffraction angle, n is order and λ is wavelength.

Resolving Power

Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely spaced spectral lines.

Resolving Power = λ / Δλ = nN

9. Polarization

Polarization is the phenomenon of restricting vibrations of light to a particular direction.

Unpolarized Light

In unpolarized light, electric field vibrations occur in all directions perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Polarized Light

In polarized light, vibrations are restricted to one particular direction.

UNPOLARIZED LIGHT ↗ ↑ ↘ → → → → → → → ↘ ↓ ↗ Many directions of vibration POLARIZED LIGHT → → → → → → → ↑ ↑ ↑ Only one direction

10. Characteristics of Lasers

LASER stands for: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

Characteristics

  • Highly monochromatic.
  • Highly coherent.
  • Highly directional.
  • Very high intensity.
  • Highly focused.

11. Spontaneous Emission

When an excited atom returns to a lower energy state by itself and emits a photon, the process is called spontaneous emission.

E₂ ───────────── ↓ ↓ Photon ↓ E₁ ───────────── Spontaneous emission

12. Stimulated Emission

When an incoming photon interacts with an excited atom and causes it to emit a second photon having the same frequency, phase and direction, it is called stimulated emission.

Incoming photon → → E₂ ───────────── ↓ ↓ E₁ ───────────── → → Two identical photons

13. Meta-stable State

A meta-stable state is an excited energy state in which atoms remain for a comparatively longer time before returning to the lower energy state.

It helps in achieving population inversion, which is necessary for laser action.

14. Population Inversion and Pumping

Population inversion is the condition in which the number of atoms in the higher energy state is greater than the number of atoms in the lower energy state.

N₂ > N₁

Pumping is the process of supplying external energy to atoms to achieve population inversion.

E₃ ───────────── ↑ ↑ Pumping ↑ E₂ ───────────── Meta-stable ↓ ↓ Laser ↓ E₁ ─────────────

15. Einstein's Coefficients

Einstein introduced three coefficients to explain the interaction between radiation and atoms.

  1. A₂₁ - coefficient of spontaneous emission.
  2. B₁₂ - coefficient of absorption.
  3. B₂₁ - coefficient of stimulated emission.
B₁₂ = B₂₁
A₂₁ / B₂₁ = 8πhν³ / c³

2 UNIT 2 - Electrostatics, Magnetostatics and EM Waves

1. Divergence of Electrostatic Field

Divergence represents the net outward flow of an electric field from a small volume.

∇ · E = ρ / ε₀

This is the differential form of Gauss's law.

2. Curl of Electrostatic Field

For an electrostatic field, the curl is zero.

∇ × E = 0

Therefore, an electrostatic field is conservative.

3. Laplace's Equation

In a charge-free region, the electrostatic potential satisfies Laplace's equation.

∇²V = 0
Exam Point: Laplace equation is applicable when charge density inside the region is zero.

4. Poisson's Equation

In a region containing charge density ρ, the electrostatic potential satisfies Poisson's equation.

∇²V = -ρ / ε₀

5. Biot-Savart Law

Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field produced by a current carrying conductor.

dB = (μ₀ / 4π) (I dl sinθ / r²)
P * /| / | / | r / | -------*---|--------- dl → Current I

6. Divergence of Magnetic Field

Magnetic field lines always form closed loops. Therefore magnetic monopoles do not exist.

∇ · B = 0

7. Curl of Static Magnetic Field

For a steady current distribution:

∇ × B = μ₀J

8. Scalar and Vector Potential

Electric and magnetic fields can be represented using scalar and vector potentials.

E = -∇V
B = ∇ × A

Stokes' theorem relates a surface integral to a line integral around its boundary.

∮ A · dl = ∫ (∇ × A) · dS

9. Continuity Equation

The continuity equation expresses conservation of electric charge.

∇ · J + ∂ρ/∂t = 0

10. Maxwell's Equations

Maxwell's equations describe electric and magnetic fields and their interaction.

∇ · E = ρ/ε₀
∇ · B = 0
∇ × E = -∂B/∂t
∇ × B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t

⭐ Very Important

Maxwell's four equations are highly important for long-answer questions.

11. Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields which propagate through space.

Electric field, magnetic field and direction of propagation are mutually perpendicular.

E ↑ │ │ │ └────────────→ Direction / / B E ⟂ B ⟂ Direction

12. Plane EM Waves in Vacuum

In vacuum, electromagnetic waves travel with the speed of light.

c = 1 / √(μ₀ε₀)
E/B = c

13. Skin Depth

Skin depth is the distance inside a conductor at which the amplitude of an electromagnetic field falls to 1/e of its surface value.

δ = √(2 / ωμσ)

At high frequencies, current tends to flow mainly near the surface of a conductor. This is called the skin effect.

14. Poynting Vector

The Poynting vector represents the rate of flow of electromagnetic energy per unit area.

S = E × H

Its direction represents the direction of propagation of electromagnetic energy.

15. Poynting Theorem

Poynting theorem represents the conservation of energy in an electromagnetic field.

It states that the decrease in electromagnetic energy stored in a volume is equal to the energy flowing out plus the energy dissipated.

3 UNIT 3 - Wave Nature of Particles and Schrödinger Equation

1. Introduction to Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the behaviour of matter and radiation at atomic and subatomic scales.

Classical physics cannot completely explain phenomena such as blackbody radiation, photoelectric effect and atomic spectra.

2. Wave Nature of Particles

Louis de Broglie proposed that moving particles such as electrons possess wave nature.

λ = h/p

For a particle of momentum p = mv:

λ = h/mv
Remember: Smaller momentum means larger de Broglie wavelength.

3. Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation describes the evolution of the quantum wave function with time.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = -ℏ²/2m ∇²ψ + Vψ

⭐ 6-Mark Question

Derive the time-dependent Schrödinger equation and explain the significance of each term.

4. Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

For a stationary state, the Schrödinger equation becomes time independent.

-ℏ²/2m ∇²ψ + Vψ = Eψ

Here ψ is the wave function, V is potential energy and E is total energy.

5. Born Interpretation

According to Born's interpretation, the square of the magnitude of the wave function represents probability density.

Probability Density = |ψ|²

6. Probability Current

Probability current represents the flow of probability associated with a quantum particle.

J = (ℏ/2mi) [ψ*∇ψ - ψ∇ψ*]

7. Expectation Value

Expectation value represents the average value of a physical quantity obtained from repeated measurements on identically prepared systems.

⟨A⟩ = ∫ ψ* A ψ dτ

8. Free Particle Wave Function

For a free particle, the potential energy is zero. The wave function can be represented as a plane wave.

ψ = A ei(kx-ωt)

9. Wave Packets

A wave packet is formed by the superposition of waves having different wavelengths and frequencies.

Wave packet /\ /\ / \ / \ ____/____\__/____\____

10. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

It is impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position and exact momentum of a particle.

Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2

Similarly:

ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2

⭐ Exam Point

State the principle, write mathematical expression and explain its physical significance.

4 UNIT 4 - Applications of Schrödinger Equation

1. Particle in a One-Dimensional Box

Consider a particle confined inside a one-dimensional box of length L. The potential energy inside the box is zero and outside the box is infinite.

where n = 1, 2, 3, ... represents the quantum number.

Wave Function

ψₙ = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)

2. Finite Potential Well

In a finite potential well, the potential energy outside the well is finite rather than infinite.

Unlike the infinite well, the wave function penetrates slightly into the classically forbidden region.

3. Quantum Tunneling

Quantum tunneling is the phenomenon in which a particle passes through a potential barrier even when its energy is less than the barrier height.

  • Tunnel diode.

4. Quantum Harmonic Oscillator

A quantum harmonic oscillator is a system in which the restoring force is proportional to displacement.

Eₙ = (n + 1/2)ℏω

where n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

The lowest energy is not zero. It is called zero-point energy.

E₀ = 1/2 ℏω

5 UNIT 5 - Crystal Structures

1. Lattice Points

Lattice points are imaginary points arranged in a regular periodic pattern representing the positions of atoms or groups of atoms in a crystal.

2. Space Lattice

A space lattice is a three-dimensional periodic arrangement of lattice points.

●────●────● /│ /│ /│ ●────●────● │ │ ●──│─●──│─● │/ │/ │/ ●────●────● 3-D lattice

3. Basis

A basis is a group of atoms associated with each lattice point.

Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis

4. Bravais Lattice

Bravais lattices are the fourteen possible distinct three-dimensional lattice arrangements.

5. Unit Cell and Lattice Parameters

A unit cell is the smallest repeating unit of a crystal lattice.

Lattice parameters are a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ that describe the geometry of a unit cell.

6. Simple Cubic (SC)

In simple cubic structure, atoms are present only at the eight corners of the cube.

  • Atoms per unit cell = 1
  • Coordination number = 6
  • Packing factor ≈ 52.4%
r = a/2

7. Body Centered Cubic (BCC)

BCC has atoms at the eight corners and one atom at the centre of the cube.

  • Atoms per unit cell = 2
  • Coordination number = 8
  • Packing factor ≈ 68%
√3a = 4r

8. Face Centered Cubic (FCC)

FCC has atoms at the eight corners and at the centre of each of the six faces.

  • Atoms per unit cell = 4
  • Coordination number = 12
  • Packing factor ≈ 74%
√2a = 4r

9. SC vs BCC vs FCC

Property SC BCC FCC
Atoms/unit cell 1 2 4
Coordination number 6 8 12
Packing factor 52.4% 68% 74%

10. Defects in Crystals

A crystal defect is an irregularity or deviation from the perfect periodic arrangement of atoms.

Classification

  • Point defects
  • Line defects
  • Surface defects
  • Volume defects

11. Frenkel Defect

In Frenkel defect, a smaller ion leaves its normal lattice position and occupies an interstitial site.

12. Schottky Defect

In Schottky defect, equal numbers of positive and negative ions are missing from their normal lattice positions.

The crystal remains electrically neutral.

6 UNIT 6 - Free Electron Theory of Metals

1. Classical Free Electron Theory

According to classical free electron theory, metals contain a large number of free electrons which move randomly inside the metal.

Postulates

  • Metal contains free electrons.
  • Positive ions remain fixed.
  • Electrons move randomly.
  • Electron collisions are assumed to be elastic.
  • Electrons obey classical mechanics.

2. Failures of Classical Free Electron Theory

  • Could not explain specific heat of metals.
  • Could not explain temperature dependence of conductivity.
  • Could not explain electronic heat capacity correctly.
  • Could not explain some magnetic properties.
  • Could not explain the distribution of electrons.

3. Quantum Free Electron Theory

Quantum free electron theory treats electrons according to quantum mechanics rather than classical mechanics.

Electrons obey Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle.

4. Fermi-Dirac Distribution

Fermi-Dirac distribution gives the probability that an available energy state is occupied by an electron.

f(E) = 1 / [e(E-EF)/kT + 1]

5. Fermi Energy

Fermi energy is the highest occupied energy level of electrons in a metal at absolute zero temperature.

EF = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3

6. Density of States

Density of states represents the number of available electron states per unit energy interval.

It helps in understanding the distribution of electrons among different energy levels.

7. Thermionic Emission

Thermionic emission is the emission of electrons from a heated metal surface when sufficient thermal energy is supplied.

Heated metal ─────────── • • • • • • ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ Electrons escaping

8. Richardson's Equation

Richardson's equation describes thermionic emission from a heated metal surface.

J = A T² e-φ/kT

Here J is thermionic emission current density, A is Richardson constant, T is absolute temperature, φ is work function and k is Boltzmann constant.
